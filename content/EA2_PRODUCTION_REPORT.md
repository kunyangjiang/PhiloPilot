## 2. Important arguments

- **Doubt:** three stages, each with what it leaves standing; the evil genius need only be unexcluded, not believed. The aim is a foundation, not tranquillity.
- **Cogito:** "if he deceives me, I exist" (AT VII 25); a five-step reconstruction; the not-a-syllogism point is labelled as a general overview; "quandiu cogito" limits its scope (AT VII 27). The Cartesian circle is set out with several readings, and the chapter states that "obviously circular" is not the scholarly consensus.
- **Innate ideas (V23 correction):** the three classes (AT VII 37–38) are shown to be provisional at that point ("nondum … clare perspexi"). The idea of God is argued to be innate by elimination (AT VII 51), reconstructed in four steps, with the weak points named (step 1 assumes the division is exhaustive; step 3's "cannot add or subtract" test). The mathematical "true and immutable natures" (AT VII 64) resist the will. Innate ideas supply the content and clear and distinct perception the standard (Samet). The gout analogy reads innateness as a disposition. Locke's challenge is set out as a five-step dilemma and cross-referenced to 《经验论、启蒙与自由主义的起源》 (EA3), and Leibniz's marble reply is given; a three-way table compares them.
- **Pascal (V23 correction):** reason's last step is to recognise its limits (fr. 267), so he is not an irrationalist. The heart knows first principles (fr. 282) and is distinguished from the heart that feels God (fr. 278). The thinking reed (fr. 347). The Wager (fr. 233) is presented as prudential, not evidential, reasoning about belief rather than a proof that God exists. It is set out as a decision table, with Hájek's three arguments (superdominance, expectation, generalised expectation), the "abêtira" advice, and the objections (many gods, probability, morality, Voltaire). Two readings are distinguished: as an argument anyone can assess (Hájek), and within Pascal's Jansenist theology (Clarke & Wood). A table separates his philosophical arguments from his religious commitments, followed by four tensions.
- **Elisabeth:** the three modes of determining motion, contact and extension (AT III 661), reconstructed in seven steps. Descartes answers with primitive notions and the weight analogy. She rejects the analogy as an unintelligible "real quality" and points to the vapours. Her own position is presented as hers, with two scholarly readings.
- **Spinoza:** one substance in three steps (no shared attribute; God has all attributes); necessity and the critique of final causes; mind and body as one thing under two attributes, so no interaction problem arises.
- **Leibniz:** simple substances; windowless monads; pre-established harmony; the mill. The theodicy runs from sufficient reason and divine perfection, not from observation, so "everything is fine" is a caricature.
- **Cavendish and Conway:** opposite grounds against mechanism, materialist vs spiritual monist. This is taken from SEP and labelled.

## 5. Critiques and responses

There are five topics:

1. The Cartesian circle, with the interpreters' responses.
2. Elisabeth's interaction problem and the later answers: Spinoza, Malebranche, Leibniz.
3. Criticisms of Spinoza's monism, starting with the argument of I p5.
4. Pre-established harmony as a "performance".
5. Evil and sufficient reason: Voltaire, and the more philosophical objections.
