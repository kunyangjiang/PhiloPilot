## 2. Important arguments

- **Bacon's three methods:** the ant, the spider and the bee. Experience is organised and methodical.
- **Locke:** no innate ideas, but an active mind; two sources of ideas; secondary qualities as powers, not resemblances.
- **Berkeley:** a valid argument whose second premise is Locke's; the representationalist reply; what the tree-in-the-park argument does and does not show.
- **Hume on induction (method focus):** the inference examined; the uniformity principle; four possible grounds, each excluded; the circle; custom as a "sceptical solution"; the limits of the conclusion.
- **Personal identity (§三, final corrections):** Locke ties personhood to consciousness, not substance, and calls "person" a forensic term; the transitivity and circularity objections (via Olson). Hume finds no constant impression of a self, describes a bundle, and explains the belief in identity by imagination, resemblance and causation; memory "discovers" rather than produces identity. In the Appendix Hume says he cannot reconcile two principles. The chapter presents the bundle theory as an unfinished account, not a solution.
- **Hume on morals:** the motivation argument (valid; premises 1 and 2 contested); is/ought; sentiment.
- **Miracles:** the comparison maxim; testimony as an empirical regularity. The chapter does not claim Hume proved miracles impossible.
- **Social contract:** Hobbes and Locke from different states of nature; Montesquieu's argument from "eternal experience"; Rousseau's general will and "forced to be free" (two readings).
- **Rights and reason:** Astell's and Wollstonecraft's internal critiques use the opponents' own premises (freedom as a fixed rule; reason as the ground of virtue). Du Châtelet appears as a participant in the empiricism/metaphysics debate, not only as Voltaire's companion.

## 9. Unresolved issues

Note numbers are those of the revised manuscript.

1. Voltaire (notes 79–80) rests wholly on SEP.
2. Bacon's idols (note 5) and Berkeley *Principles* §8 (note 15) were not checked in the original.
3. Personal identity: the two objections to the memory criterion follow Olson's SEP entry (note 36); Reid's and Butler's texts were not read. The three directions for interpreting Hume's Appendix difficulty (note 43) follow general overviews; no commentator was read in the original. Hume's and Locke's own passages were checked.
4. Hobbes' "covenants without the sword" and Locke's three defects of the state of nature (notes 60, 64) rest on general overviews, and the wording is softened accordingly.
5. In 后世批判与回应, Reid, "Of the Original Contract" and the Enlightenment's relation to colonialism and slavery rest on general overviews and need a specialist.
6. Astell's political position (Tory) is stated only as a general description.
7. The structure of Hume's induction argument and the sense of "reason" are contested. The chapter follows the reading Qu & Radcliffe give and states the alternatives.
8. **Cross-references.** The subsection points to 《近代早期理性主义与新科学》 (EA2) 第二部分 for the Cartesian self, 《康德》 (EA4) 第二部分 for the "I think", and 《早期佛教与阿毗达磨》 (IN2) 第三部分 for no-self. The brief named IN3 for no-self; the no-self and personal-identity discussion is in IN2 §三 (无我与人格同一性), so the chapter points there. None of those courses was changed.
