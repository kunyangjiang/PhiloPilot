## 2. Central philosophical arguments

- **Three separate claims.** Contact, transmission and influence must be argued for separately. Evidence for the first does not carry over to the third.
- **A ladder of evidence.** Explicit testimony and textual dependence rank above a plausible route, which ranks above mere similarity. Five questions apply to any claim: chronology, route, textual evidence, alternative explanations, and proportion between claim and evidence.
- **The matrix.** One historical relationship can contain claims of very different strength. "Aśoka had contact with Greek kings" is documented; "Aśoka's 法 influenced Greek philosophy" has no evidence. The two are often run together.
- **Misreading.** A rewrite of the Leibniz sentence shows almost every word needing correction:
  - Leibniz developed binary arithmetic in about 1674–1679; a manuscript is dated 15 March 1679.
  - That was more than twenty years before Bouvet's letter of 1701.
  - Bouvet, not Leibniz, first read the hexagram arrangement as binary numbers. Leibniz accepted the reading as confirmation of his own discovery.
  - Whether the authors of the *Yijing* knew binary is a separate question, and the evidence does not support it.

## 5. Comparative methodology

This chapter is about the method itself. It applies D1's rule (similarity is not influence) to five historical cases. It distinguishes documented, reconstructed, school-level and speculative claims. It gives the reader an explicit matrix rather than a verdict on any tradition's originality.
