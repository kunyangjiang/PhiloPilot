## 0. Owner-approved principles (APPROVED)

Fewer courses; substantially greater depth per course; continuous philosophical narratives; guided primary-text reading; active reasoning. A course is a substantial coherent chapter organized around a major problem, intellectual development or system. Depth over count; historical and intellectual continuity; arguments over biographies; primary texts, interpretation, objections and responses integrated; interactions only where they materially improve understanding; course length varies; structured progression plus free exploration. (Full text in the direction file §1.)

Also approved by the Product Owner: the four-domain architecture (A–D); the **72-course planning baseline** (not a cap; changes need a concrete academic reason); **Chinese as the primary instructional language**, retaining original philosophical terms and necessary English; and **Critiques & Responses (后世批判与回应)** as an optional instructional pattern (§2).

The detailed course list below remains **proposal** until the Product Owner accepts it.

**Tiers (proposal).** The tier marks a recommended *foundational learning path*, not the academic importance of a tradition or course.
- **● Core (44):** the recommended foundational path.
- **◐ Core-elective (5):** JW1, AF3, JP1, KR1, LA1. The recommendation is to study **at least two that belong to meaningfully different traditions**, so the path does not suggest that East Asia means only China, that the Americas have no philosophy, or that Jewish and Africana thought are marginal. For orientation only, the five fall into four loose groupings: Jewish philosophy in its Arabic, European and modern settings (JW1); Africana and Atlantic-diaspora thought (AF3); East Asian traditions beyond China (JP1, KR1 — together one grouping); Latin American thought (LA1). The groupings are a reading aid, not a classification: they overlap (LA1 and AF3 share colonial and Atlantic themes; JW1 overlaps the Islamic and European courses), so students may reasonably count their own choices differently. This is guidance, not a completion gate; nothing is locked.
- **○ Extended (23):** deeper or specialist courses, fully part of the curriculum and open to free exploration.

Classification changes in this revision: promoted from ○ to ● — CN5 (Chinese Buddhism), EA5 (German Idealism); ● by design in the split courses — IN5 (Mīmāṃsā), IN6 (Vedānta), CN7, EA10, S10 (philosophy of science); moved from ○ to ◐ — JW1, AF3, JP1, KR1, LA1. Deliberately left ○ — IN7, IN8, CN8, EA7, EA11, EA12, IA4, IA5, AF2, S3, S8, S11, S13, GR4, A2, A4, D2, D4, D5, D7–D10. Core means the foundational path, so these are not "less important".

## 2. Cross-cutting design rules (proposal)

- **Method inside every course.** Each course names a method focus and includes guided primary-text reading, an objection–reply stretch and a closing question for independent judgment. Foundations courses supply explicit tools but are not a gate.
- **Free exploration; no hard locks.** No course is technically locked. Three entry routes (by tradition/period, by problem, by method) reach the same courses. Course notes use three labels: **recommended** (background the lesson prose will lightly assume), **helpful** (supplementary reading) and **intellectual dependency** (the course argues against or builds directly on the other; marked explicitly and rare). Domain D courses state the background they *assume*. A Core course lists only Core, Core-elective or Domain A courses as recommended; an Extended course appears for a Core course only as "helpful".
- **Critiques & Responses (后世批判与回应) — optional pattern, approved by the Product Owner.** Courses explain not only what philosophers argued but how it was later criticized, defended, revised and reinterpreted. Two cases are kept apart. (A) *Debates essential to intellectual development* — for example Marx's critique and transformation of Hegel, or documented debates within Chinese and Indian traditions — belong in the **main narrative**. (B) *Later criticism and reinterpretation* may appear in an optional, concise section titled `后世批判与回应 (Critiques & Responses)` (typically about 400–800 Chinese characters, not a requirement) with four elements: 1 the later critic and the precise position criticized; 2 the critic's central argument and evidence; 3 a documented response or a clearly identified alternative interpretation, when available; 4 an unresolved question for the learner. Constraints: not required in every course; no new domain; no courses added for it; do not repeat critiques already covered in the main narrative; label documented responses separately from retrospective comparisons; present criticism fairly without implying every objection has equal scholarly support; never invent responses or debates. Candidate topics now noted in CN1, GR2, EA4 and EA6 are **unverified** and are decided, with sources checked, at authoring time.
- **Method focus.** Every course names one concise method focus (what the learner practises), drawn from a small set: argument reconstruction, conceptual analysis, primary-text interpretation, comparison of competing theories, evaluation of objections, source criticism. It is a statement of emphasis, not a count of 72 separate interactions.
- **Reading sections** are working outlines (typically 5–8), not a quota.
- **Appearance rule.** A figure may recur only where purpose differs: in B for context and development; in C as an argument used on a problem; in D as one side of a comparative debate. Each C and D course lists what is *not* repeated.
- **Women and feminist philosophy**, **non-Western voices** and **modern/contemporary** work are integrated into B courses where the history includes them (see the table in §6), plus a dedicated advanced course D6.
- **Claims of direct response** between philosophers must be tagged (§8.3) before any lesson is written.

## 3. Domain A — Foundations and Methods (4)

### A1 ● What Is Philosophy? Questions, Arguments and Reading
- **Problem:** what philosophical questions are, how arguments work, how to read a philosophical text with charity.
- **Scope:** kinds of philosophical question; premises, conclusions, validity, strength; common fallacies and cognitive traps; reconstructing a thesis; objections and replies. Examples drawn from several traditions (Plato's *Euthyphro*, a Mohist canon, a Nyāya inference, a passage of Mencius) [U].
- **Debates:** good argument vs persuasion; deductive vs inductive strength; whether philosophy makes progress.
- **Sections:** 1 Questions that stay open · 2 Arguments and their parts · 3 Good and bad inference (fallacies) · 4 Reading for the question a text answers · 5 Objection and reply · 6 Forming and defending a view.
- **Method focus:** argument reconstruction (premises, conclusion, hidden assumptions).
- **Outcomes:** identify and reconstruct a short argument; name the main fallacies; summarize a passage fairly; state an objection.
- **Prereq/links:** none. Feeds every course. Reference for fallacies/critical thinking: SEP *fallacies*, *critical-thinking* [URL].
- **Lineage:** merges old M1, M2, M3, M4.

### A2 ○ Formal Reasoning: Propositional and Predicate Logic
- **Problem:** what makes an inference valid in virtue of form.
- **Scope:** truth tables, natural deduction, quantifiers, identity, translation between English and formal language, limits of formalization.
- **Debates:** classical vs non-classical logics; how much natural language can be formalized.
- **Sections:** 1 Propositions and connectives · 2 Truth tables · 3 Deduction · 4 Quantifiers · 5 Translation and its limits · 6 What logic cannot settle.
- **Method focus:** formal evaluation of validity, and judging when formalization distorts.
- **Outcomes:** formalize and test arguments; judge when formalization helps or distorts.
- **Prereq/links:** A1 recommended. Used by S11, S6, IN3, CN2.
- **Lineage:** merges M8, M9. Whether it is in scope is an open decision (§9, item 4).

### A3 ● Concepts, Cases and Thought Experiments
- **Problem:** how philosophers define, test and refine concepts.
- **Scope:** definitions, necessary/sufficient conditions, counterexamples, family resemblance, explication; thought experiments (e.g. Zhuangzi's butterfly, ring of Gyges, Mary's room, trolley cases [U]) and how to vary and evaluate them; intuition and its critics.
- **Debates:** definition vs family resemblance; how far intuitions can be trusted.
- **Sections:** 1 Defining and its failures · 2 Counterexample method · 3 Family resemblance and vague concepts · 4 What a thought experiment tests · 5 Varying a case · 6 Doubts about intuition.
- **Method focus:** conceptual analysis by definition and counterexample.
- **Outcomes:** test a definition; design a variation; say what a case is meant to show.
- **Prereq/links:** A1 recommended. Feeds S2, S4, S7.
- **Lineage:** merges M5, M6.

### A4 ○ Interpretation, Translation and Philosophical Writing
- **Problem:** how to understand a text across language, culture and time, and how to write a philosophical argument.
- **Scope:** intention vs text, anachronism, commentary traditions, translation choices (one term, several translations), thesis/objection/reply writing, citation.
- **Debates:** authorial intention vs the text itself; literal vs idiomatic translation.
- **Sections:** 1 Text, author, reader · 2 Context and anachronism · 3 Commentary as philosophy · 4 What translation does (e.g. *dao*, *dharma*, *logos* [U]) · 5 Building an argument in writing · 6 Giving and receiving critique.
- **Method focus:** comparing translations and commentary readings of one passage.
- **Outcomes:** compare translations; write a structured argumentative response.
- **Prereq/links:** A1. Used by D1, the text-study modules, D11.
- **Lineage:** merges M7, M10; absorbs the "method across traditions" idea of M12.

## 4. Domain B — Global History of Philosophy (44)

Each course is a continuous narrative driven by named debates. Figures are participants, not subjects of biography.

### B1. Greek and Roman (4)

#### GR1 ● Origins of Greek Philosophy: From Nature to the Examined Life
- **Problem:** how did explanation of the world and the question of how to live become critical inquiry.
- **Scope:** Presocratics (Milesians, Heraclitus, Parmenides, atomists), Sophists, Socrates.
- **Debates:** change vs being; nature vs convention; whether virtue is knowledge.
- **Sections:** 1 First accounts of nature · 2 Parmenides and the problem of change · 3 Atomism and pluralism · 4 Sophists, rhetoric and relativism · 5 Socratic method · 6 Trial and the examined life.
- **Method focus:** interpreting fragmentary sources; reconstructing Parmenides' argument from fragments.
- **Outcomes:** reconstruct Parmenides' argument; distinguish elenchus from lecture; discuss the Sophist challenge.
- **Prereq/links:** none. → GR2, S7.
- **Lineage:** merges TG1, TG5; opening of the Socrates part of TG2.

#### GR2 ● Plato: Knowledge, Soul and the Just City
- **Problem:** what is knowledge, what is justice, and what must reality be like for either to exist.
- **Scope:** early, middle and late dialogues; theory of Forms; *Meno*, *Phaedo*, *Republic*, *Theaetetus*, *Parmenides* (selection) [U]. Diotima's speech in the *Symposium* and ancient reception of women in philosophy [U].
- **Debates:** recollection, Forms and the "third man"; justice in soul and city; knowledge as true belief with an account.
- **Sections:** 1 Definitions and the *Euthyphro* problem · 2 Recollection and Forms · 3 Soul and immortality · 4 Justice in the soul and city · 5 Knowledge and appearance · 6 Plato's self-criticism · 7 Reading a dialogue as philosophy.
- **Method focus:** reconstructing arguments from dialogue form; separating speaker, character and author.
- **Critiques & Responses (optional, candidate):** later criticism of Plato's political philosophy [unverified; critic and position to be chosen and checked at authoring]. Format and rules in §2.
- **Outcomes:** reconstruct the *Republic* argument for justice; explain a major objection to Forms.
- **Prereq/links:** GR1 recommended. → S1, S9, D3.
- **Lineage:** TG2.

#### GR3 ● Aristotle: Substance, Causes, Logic and the Good Life
- **Problem:** what is a thing, how do we know, and what is human flourishing.
- **Scope:** *Categories*, *Physics*, *Metaphysics*, *De Anima*, *Nicomachean Ethics*, *Politics*, *Organon*; early commentary tradition.
- **Debates:** substance vs Forms; four causes; syllogistic; function argument; virtue and practical wisdom.
- **Sections:** 1 Critique of Plato · 2 Syllogistic logic · 3 Substance and change · 4 Causes and nature · 5 Soul and perception · 6 Virtue and *eudaimonia* · 7 The city.
- **Method focus:** working a technical vocabulary (substance, cause) and applying the syllogism.
- **Outcomes:** use the syllogism; reconstruct the function argument; compare with Plato.
- **Prereq/links:** GR2 recommended. → IA2 (reception), EA1, S7.
- **Lineage:** TG3.

#### GR4 ○ Hellenistic and Roman Schools: Living Philosophy
- **Problem:** how to live and judge under uncertainty after the classical city.
- **Scope:** Stoics (Zeno, Epictetus, Seneca, Marcus Aurelius), Epicurus and Lucretius, Academic and Pyrrhonian skeptics, Cynics; Hipparchia and women in the schools [U].
- **Debates:** fate and freedom; pleasure and tranquility; skepticism and action; passions as judgments.
- **Sections:** 1 New settings · 2 Epicurean physics and ethics · 3 Stoic logic and ethics · 4 Fate and responsibility · 5 Skeptical modes · 6 Philosophy as therapy.
- **Method focus:** evaluating therapeutic claims and the structure of skeptical modes.
- **Outcomes:** reconstruct a skeptical mode; compare Stoic and Epicurean tranquility.
- **Prereq/links:** GR3 recommended. → EA1, S1, D3.
- **Lineage:** TG4.

### B2. Chinese (8)

#### CN1 ● Confucius, Mencius, Xunzi: Cultivation, Ritual and Human Nature
- **Problem:** how to become good and order society through ritual and virtue.
- **Scope:** *Analects*, *Mencius*, *Xunzi*; Zisi/*Zhongyong* tradition; Ban Zhao later [U]. Context from SEP [URL: confucius, ethics-chinese].
- **Debates:** *ren*, *li*, *yi*; nature good (Mencius) vs bad (Xunzi); heart-mind (*xin*); moral education.
- **Sections:** 1 The Zhou inheritance · 2 Analects: virtue and ritual · 3 Mencius: sprouts and the heart-mind · 4 Xunzi: ritual and shaping · 5 Rival readings of *ren* · 6 Politics of virtue.
- **Method focus:** conceptual analysis of thick terms (*ren*, *li*) across translations; comparing rival theories of human nature.
- **Critiques & Responses (optional, candidate):** later critiques of Confucian ethics (e.g. those associated with Deng Xiaomang) [unverified; check the original scholarship at authoring]. Format and rules in §2.
- **Outcomes:** reconstruct Mencius' ox-mountain argument and state a Xunzian counter-position (a direct reply is not asserted, see §8.3); discuss translation of *ren*.
- **Prereq/links:** none. → CN6, S7, D4.
- **Lineage:** TC1, TC2.

#### CN2 ● Mohism, Names and the Later Mohist Canons: Argument, Language and Science
- **Problem:** how to justify morality by consequences and how to argue about names and reality.
- **Scope:** Mozi (impartial concern, anti-fatalism), Later Mohist Canons (language, logic, optics, mechanics), School of Names (Hui Shi, Gongsun Long) [U, V-TOC: Mohism, Mohist Canons, logic and language in early Chinese philosophy; URL: mohist-canons, chinese-logic-language].
- **Debates:** impartial concern vs graded love; "white horse is not horse"; Mohist logic vs Aristotelian logic; truth and disputation (*bian*).
- **Sections:** 1 Mohist ethics and politics · 2 Standards of judgment · 3 Names and objects · 4 Disputation and its rules · 5 Optics and mechanics · 6 The School of Names · 7 Why Mohism declined.
- **Method focus:** reading terse canon statements as arguments; testing definitions.
- **Outcomes:** read a canon passage as argument; compare with Aristotelian and Nyāya methods.
- **Prereq/links:** CN1 recommended. → D3, S6.
- **Lineage:** TC3; part of M11, C4.

#### CN3 ● Daoist Traditions: Laozi, Zhuangzi and Their Readers
- **Problem:** what is the right relation between human order and the spontaneous way of things.
- **Scope:** *Laozi/Dao De Jing*, *Zhuangzi* (inner chapters), *Huainanzi* (selection), Huang-Lao thought [U].
- **Debates:** *dao* and *de*; *wuwei*; skepticism and perspectivism; transformation and death; critique of Confucian and Mohist norms.
- **Sections:** 1 The *Laozi* as text and layers · 2 *Dao* and *wuwei* · 3 Zhuangzi: perspective and skepticism · 4 Language and "forgetting" · 5 Life, death, transformation · 6 Early commentary and Huang-Lao.
- **Method focus:** interpreting a multi-voiced, layered text; separating argument from literary form.
- **Outcomes:** reconstruct the "butterfly" and "happy fish" passages as argument; discuss limits of "mystical" readings.
- **Prereq/links:** CN1 recommended. → CN5, D3.
- **Lineage:** TC4.

#### CN4 ● Legalism, the Han Synthesis and Correlative Cosmology
- **Problem:** how should rulers govern and how does the cosmos relate to the state.
- **Scope:** Shang Yang, Shen Buhai, Han Feizi; Qin–Han state; Dong Zhongshu and *yin–yang*/five-phases cosmology; *Yijing* and philosophy of change; Wang Chong's critique [U; V-TOC: Legalism, Han Dynasty, philosophy of change; URL: chinese-legalism, chinese-change, chinese-social-political].
- **Debates:** law/technique/position; human nature and incentives; Heaven and portents; naturalism vs correlative cosmology.
- **Sections:** 1 State-building and the question of order · 2 Han Feizi's three tools · 3 Qin to Han: Confucianization · 4 Correlative cosmology · 5 *Yijing* and change · 6 Wang Chong's skepticism.
- **Method focus:** comparing competing theories of government; evaluating correlative explanation.
- **Outcomes:** compare Legalist and Confucian conceptions of authority; evaluate correlative explanation.
- **Prereq/links:** CN1 recommended. → S9, D5.
- **Lineage:** TC5, TC6.

#### CN5 ● Wei-Jin Metaphysics and Chinese Buddhism: Emptiness, Nature and Sudden Awakening
- **Problem:** how a foreign philosophy was reconstructed in Chinese terms, and with what philosophical effect.
- **Scope:** *Xuanxue* (Wang Bi, Guo Xiang), early translation problems (*geyi*), Sengzhao, Tiantai, Huayan, Chan [V-TOC: Tiantai, Huayan, Chan Buddhism].
- **Debates:** being and non-being; Buddha-nature; sudden vs gradual; part–whole in Huayan.
- **Sections:** 1 Wei-Jin "learning of the mystery" · 2 Translation and matching concepts · 3 Sengzhao and emptiness · 4 Tiantai threefold truth · 5 Huayan interpenetration · 6 Chan and anti-scholasticism.
- **Method focus:** tracing one concept through translation and reconstruction.
- **Outcomes:** trace one concept from Indian source to Chinese reconstruction (for the two-truths distinction see §8.1).
- **Prereq/links:** CN3 and IN4 recommended. → CN6, D10.
- **Lineage:** TC7.

#### CN6 ● Neo-Confucianism to Qing: Principle, Mind and Practical Learning
- **Problem:** what is the relation of principle (*li*), vital force (*qi*), mind and action.
- **Scope:** Zhou Dunyi, Zhang Zai, Cheng brothers, Zhu Xi, Lu Jiuyuan, Wang Yangming, Wang Fuzhi, Dai Zhen, evidential scholarship, Qing critique of Neo-Confucian rigor [V-TOC: Song-Ming Confucianism, Qing philosophy].
- **Debates:** *li*–*qi*; investigation of things vs the mind is principle; unity of knowledge and action; critique of "principle kills people".
- **Sections:** 1 Response to Buddhism · 2 Cosmology and ontology of *li* and *qi* · 3 Zhu Xi's investigation · 4 Wang Yangming's mind · 5 Knowledge and action · 6 Qing empiricism and the critique of *li* · 7 Women's learning and education [U].
- **Method focus:** comparing two systems on a shared question; reading commentary as philosophy.
- **Outcomes:** contrast Zhu and Wang; evaluate the unity of knowledge and action.
- **Prereq/links:** CN1, CN5 recommended. → CN7.
- **Lineage:** TC8 (first half) + new Qing.

#### CN7 ● Chinese Philosophy and Modernity: Late Qing Reform to the New Culture Movement
- **Problem:** how to rethink tradition, knowledge and the very idea of "Chinese philosophy" under Western and Japanese impact (c. 1890s–1920s).
- **Scope:** Kang Youwei, Tan Sitong, Yan Fu, Liang Qichao, Zhang Binglin, Liang Shuming, May Fourth critics (Chen Duxiu, Hu Shi), the 1923 science and "view of life" debate, early histories of Chinese philosophy as a new discipline (Hu Shi, Feng Youlan) [U for all].
- **Debates:** reform vs revolution; reinterpreting Confucianism as religion or as ethics; translation of Western terms; science and metaphysics; whether "philosophy" is a Chinese category.
- **Sections:** 1 Crisis and the search for causes · 2 Translating Western philosophy (new vocabulary) · 3 Reformist Confucianism · 4 Anti-traditionalism and the New Culture Movement · 5 Science and the view of life · 6 Writing the first histories of Chinese philosophy.
- **Method focus:** evaluating reinterpretation and historiographical argument.
- **Outcomes:** explain why "Chinese philosophy" became a discipline; assess one side of the science and metaphysics debate; read a reformist reinterpretation of a classical idea.
- **Prereq/links:** CN6 recommended. → CN8, D1, D6.
- **Lineage:** first half of the earlier CN7 (split: the earlier course covered 1840s to the present).

#### CN8 ○ Philosophical Systems, Marxism and Contemporary Chinese Philosophy (1930s–present)
- **Problem:** how philosophical systems were built from classical and Western resources, and how they diverged under different political and institutional settings.
- **Scope:** Feng Youlan's new principle-learning, Jin Yuelin, He Lin, Xiong Shili and the New Confucians (Tang Junyi, Mou Zongsan), Chinese Marxist philosophy (e.g. Mao's essays on practice and contradiction, Ai Siqi), post-1949 and reform-era debates, the later Confucian revival and recent analytic work on classical texts [U for all].
- **Debates:** moral metaphysics and "intellectual intuition"; whether Chinese thought can ground science and democracy; practice and truth; Marxism and tradition.
- **Sections:** 1 System-building in the 1930s–40s · 2 Logic and metaphysics (Jin Yuelin) · 3 New Confucianism · 4 Marxist philosophy in China · 5 After 1949 and reform-era debates · 6 Contemporary Confucian and analytic work.
- **Method focus:** assessing system-building arguments against their classical and Western sources.
- **Outcomes:** compare a modern system with its classical sources; assess a New Confucian argument.
- **Prereq/links:** CN7 recommended; CN6 helpful. → D1, D6.
- **Lineage:** second half of the earlier CN7.

### B3. Indian (8)

#### IN1 ● Origins and Early Debates: Vedic Thought, Upaniṣads and the Śramaṇa Schools
- **Problem:** what is the self and the cosmos, and which practices lead to liberation.
- **Scope:** Ṛg Veda hymns (selection), Brāhmaṇas, Upaniṣads (Chāndogya, Bṛhadāraṇyaka), Gārgī and Maitreyī [U], Jain thought (Mahāvīra, *anekāntavāda*), Ājīvikas, Cārvāka/Lokāyata materialism, Buddha's context.
- **Debates:** ritual vs knowledge; *ātman*–Brahman; determinism and karma; many-sidedness; materialist skepticism about inference.
- **Sections:** 1 Ritual and cosmos · 2 Upaniṣadic inquiry · 3 Karma and rebirth · 4 Jain pluralism · 5 Materialist and fatalist challengers · 6 Debate culture.
- **Method focus:** presenting rival positions fairly; reconstructing arguments from dialogue.
- **Outcomes:** present the heterodox positions as arguments; explain *anekāntavāda*.
- **Prereq/links:** none. → IN2, IN5, IN6, IN7.
- **Lineage:** TI1, TI2 (first half), TI8.

#### IN2 ● Early Buddhism and Abhidharma: Suffering, No-Self and Dependent Origination
- **Problem:** what is a person if there is no enduring self, and what ends suffering.
- **Scope:** Pali discourses [URL: ethics-indian-buddhism, mind-indian-buddhism, V-TOC: Abhidharma], Theravāda and Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma, Pudgalavāda, the Milindapañha, ethics and the path, Therīgāthā [U].
- **Debates:** no-self vs persistent person; momentariness; dharmas and their reality; conventional vs ultimate in the Abhidharma sense (§8.1).
- **Sections:** 1 The four truths · 2 Dependent origination · 3 No-self and its arguments · 4 Abhidharma analysis · 5 Momentariness and causation · 6 Ethics and mind · 7 Early women's voices.
- **Method focus:** testing reductive analysis (person into parts) and its objections.
- **Outcomes:** reconstruct the chariot argument; explain the Abhidharma form of the conventional/ultimate distinction (not "the" two-truths doctrine, see §8.1).
- **Prereq/links:** IN1 recommended. → IN3, IN4, S4, D2.
- **Lineage:** TI3.

#### IN3 ● Knowledge, Inference and Debate: Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika and the Buddhist Logicians
- **Problem:** how do we know, and what makes an inference or a debate move legitimate.
- **Scope:** Nyāya-sūtra, Vātsyāyana, Vaiśeṣika ontology, Dignāga, Dharmakīrti [URL: logic-india, epistemology-india, dharmakiirti], Udayana, Navya-Nyāya (Gaṅgeśa) [V-TOC]. Includes Mīmāṃsā epistemology debates.
- **Debates:** perception and its conceptual content; the structure of inference and universal pervasion; *apoha* vs universals; verbal testimony; theory of fallacies.
- **Sections:** 1 *Pramāṇa* theory · 2 Perception · 3 Inference and pervasion · 4 Dignāga's reform · 5 Dharmakīrti and *apoha* · 6 Nyāya objections in the tradition · 7 Navya-Nyāya precision.
- **Method focus:** formalizing inferences and applying fallacy theory.
- **Outcomes:** analyse a five-membered inference; explain the Buddhist–Nyāya dispute on universals.
- **Prereq/links:** A1 recommended; A2 helpful. → S1, S6, D3.
- **Lineage:** TI4, TI6; part of M11.

#### IN4 ● Mahāyāna Philosophy: Madhyamaka and Yogācāra
- **Problem:** what is emptiness, and does the world exist apart from mind.
- **Scope:** Prajñāpāramitā, Nāgārjuna, Āryadeva, Candrakīrti, Bhāviveka, Asaṅga, Vasubandhu [URL: madhyamaka]; Śāntideva.
- **Debates:** emptiness and the Madhyamaka and Yogācāra readings of the two truths (§8.1); whether Madhyamaka has a thesis; mind-only vs external objects; bodhisattva ethics.
- **Sections:** 1 Perfection of wisdom · 2 Nāgārjuna's fourfold negation · 3 Svātantrika/Prāsaṅgika · 4 Vasubandhu's idealist arguments · 5 Consciousness and storehouse · 6 Compassion and ethics.
- **Method focus:** reading dialectical negation as argument and evaluating objections.
- **Outcomes:** reconstruct a Madhyamaka argument; compare with Berkeley or Kant.
- **Prereq/links:** IN2. → CN5, O (Tibetan, reserved), D2, D3.
- **Lineage:** TI5.

#### IN5 ● Authority, Language and Duty: Mīmāṃsā
- **Problem:** what makes the Veda authoritative, how does language give knowledge of duty, and what is the status of testimony.

## 6. Domain D — Advanced Comparative and Applied (11 courses, plus one uncounted module series)

Domain D is not uniformly comparative. Its courses fall into three kinds:
- **Comparative (D1, D2, D3, D4, D5, D10):** a sustained debate or problem with primary readings from at least two traditions, not a table of similarities. The editorial rules for comparison (direction file §6) apply.
- **Contemporary and applied (D6, D7, D8, D9):** organized around a contemporary problem or critique. Other traditions enter where they bear on the problem and are not required for every topic (D6 is a critique of the canon; D7–D9 are applied ethics).
- **Capstone (D11):** a single-tradition or comparative project, whichever the question justifies.

Prerequisites are **recommended** background; none is enforced.

#### D1 ● Comparative Philosophy: Methods and Pitfalls
- **Problem:** can traditions be compared fairly and what distorts comparison.
- **Scope:** translation, incommensurability, projection, orientalism, "comparative" as method [V-TOC lists a Chinese–Western comparative entry; exact URL unconfirmed]; case study of "Is there Chinese or Indian philosophy?"
- **Debates:** incommensurability vs shared problems; orientalism and self-orientalism.
- **Sections:** 1 Why compare · 2 Translation and incommensurability · 3 Orientalism and self-orientalism · 4 Models of comparison · 5 A case study · 6 Rules.
- **Method focus:** detecting hidden assumptions in a proposed comparison.
- **Outcomes:** identify the assumptions of a proposed comparison; apply the editorial rules for comparison to a case.
- **Assumed background:** two B courses from different traditions; A4 helpful.

#### D2 ○ Self, Person and No-Self
- **Problem:** whether a persisting self exists, and its ethical consequences.
- **Scope:** Buddhist, Humean, Akan and Confucian views; Parfit-style reductionism [U].
- **Debates:** reductionist vs non-reductionist views of the self; relational persons.
- **Sections:** 1 The question · 2 No-self arguments · 3 Bundle views · 4 Relational persons · 5 Ethics of selflessness · 6 Evaluation.

#### D11 ● Independent Philosophical Project (capstone)
- **Problem:** the student's own question.
- **Two equally valid tracks:**
  1. **Single-tradition investigation:** a substantial study of one problem, text or debate within a tradition, pursued in depth with that tradition's own sources and secondary scholarship.
  2. **Comparative project:** a defined problem examined through texts from at least two traditions, following the editorial rules for comparison (direction file §6).
- **Deliverable:** a stated question, a reconstructed argument, the strongest objection and reply, and the student's own position.
- **Assumed background (recommended only):** A1 recommended; A4 and D1 helpful where relevant; for track 1, at least two courses in the chosen tradition; for track 2, at least one course in each tradition studied.
- **Scope:** a defined philosophical question, text or debate, chosen by the student and agreed to be answerable with the sources available. Comparison is **not** required and must not be forced; a comparison is justified only when the question itself needs two traditions.
- **Debates:** chosen by the student; the project must identify the main positions and the strongest objection to the student's own.
- **Sections:** 1 Choosing and narrowing the question · 2 Reconstructing the sources · 3 Building the argument · 4 Objection and reply · 5 Revision and independent position.
- **Method focus:** the student chooses and justifies the method for the question (argument reconstruction, interpretation, comparison or source criticism).
- **Outcomes:** deliver a stated question, a reconstructed argument, an objection and reply, and an independent position, in either track.

## 8. Academic cautions (flagged for later source checking)

These are recorded, not resolved. Nothing in this phase is a comprehensive literature audit.

### 8.1 The two-truths distinction in Buddhist philosophy
- **Caution:** there is no single "two truths doctrine". Conventional and ultimate truth are understood differently across schools, and scholars also dispute whether the distinction is about two kinds of *truth* (semantic), two kinds of *reality* (ontological) or both. The meanings differ between: Abhidharma ontologies (composites conventional; irreducible *dharmas* ultimate), Madhyamaka (Nāgārjuna and later disputes between Candrakīrti's and Bhāviveka's followers), Yogācāra, Buddhist epistemology, and the Chinese reconstructions in CN5 (e.g. Tiantai's "threefold truth").
- **Support found [S]:** the SEP entry "The Theory of Two Truths in India" (<https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/twotruths-india/>) appeared in search results; its outline includes Abhidharma, Sautrāntika, Madhyamaka (Svātantrika and Prāsaṅgika) and Yogācāra sections, and the search summary said the schools differ, sometimes radically, and that the theory can be read ontologically or semantically. The entry text itself was not read.
- **Curriculum treatment:** IN2 presents only the Abhidharma form of the distinction; IN4 presents Madhyamaka and Yogācāra readings as separate; CN5 presents the Chinese reworking separately; no course states "the" two truths as settled. IN2's earlier outcome "state the two truths" has been replaced accordingly.
- **To check later:** the origin of the distinction in the Abhidharma tradition (one search summary calls it an Abhidharma innovation that began as a heuristic — unverified); whether "Svātantrika/Prāsaṅgika" are Indian terms or later Tibetan classifications [U]; Yogācāra's position (not seen in the search results).

### 8.2 The Ethiopian *Ḥatäta* (Zära Yaʿǝqob)
- **Caution:** the authorship, authenticity and date of the *Ḥatäta* are contested. Do not present it as an unproblematic seventeenth-century text by a named author, or as the "African Descartes".
- **Support found [S] (secondary summaries only):** Carlo Conti Rossini questioned the text from 1920, proposing that the Capuchin missionary Giusto da Urbino wrote it; Eugen Mittwoch is reported to have supported this in 1934; Claude Sumner rejected the forgery claim in 1976, citing letters of Giusto da Urbino; sources note a political valence to the debate in the Italian colonial setting; a Cambridge repository item and a De Gruyter volume on the "authorship question" appeared. One summary says most scholars now accept Zära Yaʿǝqob's authorship, but that summary rests partly on an encyclopaedia page and may lag current scholarship. Primary scholarship was not read.
- **Curriculum treatment:** AF1 presents the *Ḥatäta* as a text whose authorship and date are disputed and teaches the dispute itself; no lesson may assert authorship or date as settled. The related *Walda Ḥeywat* is not discussed in the course until verified. Spelling variants (Hatata/Ḥatäta) should be normalized after checking an edition.
- **To check later:** current state of the scholarship; reliable translations and rights; whether the text should appear in AF1 as a primary text or only as a case study.

### 8.3 Claims that one philosopher directly answered another
- **Rule:** before any lesson is written, each "X replied to Y", "X's critique of Y" or "X's response" must be tagged **[documented]** (the later author names or clearly targets the earlier one), **[reconstruction]** (scholars infer it from content and context) or **[school-level]** (a school's position, not one named person's reply). Otherwise use neutral wording such as "rival position" or "a later objection in this tradition".
- **Edits made now:** CN1 outcome no longer says "Xunzi's reply" to Mencius' ox-mountain argument (now: a Xunzian counter-position); IN3 section "Nyāya replies" now reads "Nyāya objections in the tradition"; IN6 now treats Rāmānuja and Madhva as responding to Advaita *as a tradition*, with the question of direct engagement with Śaṅkara marked for checking.
- **Entries to check (not exhaustive):** CN2 (Later Mohists and the School of Names: [reconstruction]); IN3 (Dignāga, Uddyotakara, Dharmakīrti, and later Nyāya authors: partly documented, partly school-level); IN5/IN6 (Mīmāṃsā vs Vedānta, Buddhist critiques of both); IA2/IA4 (Ṣadrā's use of Avicenna: [documented in part]); CN6 (Wang Yangming vs Zhu Xi: [documented] but fine details to check); IA3 (Averroes' *Incoherence of the Incoherence* names Ghazālī's work in its title: [documented], but the detailed point-by-point framing is not verified).
- **Documented in principle but still to be checked in detail:** Elisabeth–Descartes correspondence (EA2); Kant's statement about Hume (EA4).
